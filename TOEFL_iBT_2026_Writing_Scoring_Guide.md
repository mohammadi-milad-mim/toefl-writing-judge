# TOEFL iBT 2026 Writing Scoring Guide

## Official Technical Reference

This guide is based primarily on the **October 2025 TOEFL iBT Technical Manual**, published as:

- **TOEFL Research Report No. RR-106**
- **ETS Research Report No. RR-25-12**
- Manna, V. F., Li, S., Papageorgiou, S., & Gu, L. (2025)

The manual describes the TOEFL design and scoring procedures introduced for the test beginning in January 2026. The public ETS Writing Scoring Guide contains the same task-level 0–5 rubrics in a shorter format.

This document separates:

1. the **official scoring system**;
2. the **official task constructs and rubric descriptors**;
3. the **automated-scoring feature categories disclosed by ETS**;
4. a practical procedure for assigning an official-style score.

---

# 1. Writing Section Scoring Structure

The Writing section contains three task types:

| Task | Number of items | Raw score range |
|---|---:|---:|
| Build a Sentence | 10 | 0–10 |
| Write an Email | 1 | 0–5 |
| Write for an Academic Discussion | 1 | 0–5 |
| **Total** | **12** | **0–20** |

The raw Writing score is therefore:

\[
\text{Build a Sentence points}
+
\text{Email score}
+
\text{Academic Discussion score}
=
\text{Writing raw score out of 20}
\]

Example:

\[
9 + 4 + 4 = 17/20
\]

## 1.1 Build a Sentence

Each Build a Sentence item is scored:

- **1**: correct;
- **0**: incorrect.

These items are key-scored. They do not use the extended-response rubrics.

## 1.2 Extended Writing Tasks

The two constructed responses are each scored holistically from **0 to 5**:

- Write an Email: 0–5
- Write for an Academic Discussion: 0–5

ETS reports that the constructed Writing responses are evaluated using its proprietary automated scoring engines together with human scoring procedures.

## 1.3 Raw Score to Reported Writing Band

The total raw Writing score is converted to the reported **1–6 section band**, in 0.5 increments, through a statistical linking procedure.

ETS uses **weighted equipercentile linking** to account for small differences in difficulty among test versions. Therefore:

- the reported band is not a simple percentage;
- the band is not calculated by averaging the three task results;
- a raw score does not necessarily map to exactly the same reported band on every test form;
- ETS does not publish one permanent universal raw-to-band table.

# 2. General Scoring Principles

## 2.1 Holistic Scoring

Each extended response receives one overall score. ETS does not publish a formula such as:

- content: 40%;
- grammar: 30%;
- vocabulary: 20%;
- organization: 10%.

The rubric describes the typical characteristics of each score level, but the final rating is holistic.

A response may be stronger in one area and weaker in another. The scorer determines which overall score level best represents the response.

## 2.2 The Rubric Describes Typical Responses

The score descriptors are not mechanical checklists. A response does not need to satisfy every descriptor perfectly.

For example:

- one spelling mistake does not automatically reduce a score;
- one strong example does not automatically produce a high score;
- answering all prompt bullets does not guarantee a 4 or 5 if the message is unclear;
- several recognizable surface errors do not automatically force a response to 3 if it remains effective and easily understood.

## 2.3 Four Central Questions

Across both tasks, the scorer is effectively considering:

1. **Relevance and task achievement:** Does the response do what the prompt requires?
2. **Elaboration and coherence:** Are the important ideas sufficiently developed and logically connected?
3. **Language facility:** Are grammar, vocabulary, sentence structures, and collocations adequate for expressing the message?
4. **Accuracy and mechanics:** Do errors in grammar, usage, spelling, punctuation, or capitalization reduce clarity or effectiveness?

The Email task also gives explicit importance to **social conventions**, such as politeness, register, organization, and appropriate formulation of requests or criticism.

## 2.4 Error Counting Is Not the Scoring Method

Errors must be evaluated by their **frequency, seriousness, and effect**.

### Local errors

A local error is easy to interpret and does not damage the larger message.

Examples:

- a recognizable misspelling;
- one missing article;
- one minor subject–verb agreement error;
- a punctuation mistake that does not alter meaning.

Several local errors may still be compatible with score 4 when the response remains generally successful.

### Global or score-damaging errors

A global error weakens task performance or forces the reader to reconstruct the intended meaning.

Examples:

- omitting a major required action;
- using an example that does not support the argument;
- writing a sentence whose meaning is unclear;
- contradicting the stated position;
- using an inappropriate tone for the recipient;
- accumulating errors until part of the response becomes difficult to interpret.

# 3. Write an Email

## 3.1 Task Purpose

The test taker receives an academic or social scenario and writes for a specific communicative purpose. The purpose may include:

- making a request;
- providing information;
- extending an invitation;
- reporting a problem;
- recommending an action;
- proposing a solution.

The response must be a connected, multi-sentence email rather than isolated phrases.

## 3.2 Official Construct

The Email task measures the ability to produce a multi-sentence text that:

1. achieves the designated communication goal while following basic social conventions;
2. is adequately elaborated, clear, and cohesive;
3. uses an appropriate range of grammar and vocabulary accurately;
4. follows English spelling, punctuation, and capitalization conventions.

## 3.3 Official Scoring Dimensions and Disclosed Feature Categories

The Technical Manual identifies four main construct areas for the automated scoring model.

### A. Content

Core question:

> Does the elaboration support the communicative purpose?

Disclosed feature examples include:

- number of sentences;
- discourse coherence;
- similarity or relevance to the prompt.

### Practical interpretation

Evaluate whether the response:

- addresses the central purpose;
- covers the important prompt requirements;
- gives enough specific information;
- remains relevant;
- connects its ideas coherently.

The number of sentences is only one model feature. More sentences do not automatically produce a better score.

---

### B. Syntactic and Lexical Variety

Core question:

> Does the response use sufficient sentence variety and appropriate, idiomatic vocabulary?

Disclosed feature examples include:

- sentence variety;
- word frequency;
- correctness of collocations.

### Practical interpretation

Evaluate:

- variety of sentence structures;
- control of statements, questions, modals, conditionals, and subordinate clauses;
- suitability of vocabulary;
- natural word combinations.

Examples of appropriate collocations:

- request an extension;
- arrange a repair;
- provide an update;
- address a concern;
- attention to detail.

Examples of inaccurate collocations:

- give a feedback;
- suggest you to repair;
- make a balance;
- bad effects on my focus.

Advanced vocabulary is not required. Appropriate and controlled vocabulary is more valuable than unnecessarily complex wording.

---

### C. Social Conventions

Core question:

> Is the message formulated appropriately for the recipient and situation?

The Technical Manual specifically identifies:

- politeness;
- register;
- organization of information;
- formulation of actions such as requests, refusals, and criticism.

Disclosed automated feature examples include:

- use of modals;
- use of hedge or politeness expressions.

### Practical interpretation

Evaluate:

- whether the greeting and closing suit the relationship;
- whether the tone is appropriately formal, neutral, or friendly;
- whether requests are polite without being vague;
- whether criticism is professional;
- whether apologies and explanations are socially appropriate;
- whether the information follows a natural email sequence.

Examples:

Too direct for a property manager:

> Fix the heating system immediately.

Appropriate:

> Could you please arrange for the heating system to be inspected as soon as possible?

For a close friend, highly formal institutional language may be unnatural. Register must match the relationship.

---

### D. Accuracy and Errors

The Technical Manual lists:

- grammaticality;
- grammatical errors;
- word and usage errors;
- mechanical errors, including spelling and punctuation.

### Practical interpretation

Evaluate:

- subject–verb agreement;
- verb tense and form;
- articles;
- singular and plural forms;
- pronouns;
- prepositions;
- word forms;
- sentence boundaries;
- spelling;
- punctuation;
- capitalization.

The decisive issue is whether errors remain limited or noticeably reduce the effectiveness of the message.

---

# 4. Write an Email: Score-Level Rubric

| Score | Official label | Technical interpretation |
|---:|---|---|
| **5** | Fully successful | The email is effective and clearly expressed. Elaboration strongly supports the purpose; sentence structures are varied and controlled; vocabulary is precise and idiomatic; social conventions are consistently appropriate; almost no meaningful lexical or grammatical errors occur beyond normal timed-writing slips. |
| **4** | Generally successful | The email is mostly effective and easily understood. It provides adequate elaboration, sufficient syntactic variety, appropriate word choice, and mostly appropriate social conventions. Lexical and grammatical errors are few or limited enough not to interfere materially with the task. |
| **3** | Partially successful | The email generally accomplishes the task, but some parts may not be fully clear or effective. Elaboration only partially supports the purpose; grammar and vocabulary show a moderate range; noticeable problems may occur in structure, word forms, idiomatic language, or social conventions. |
| **2** | Mostly unsuccessful | The response attempts the task but is mostly ineffective. Development may be limited or irrelevant, language range may be restricted, and accumulated sentence-level or usage errors may make the message difficult to interpret. |
| **1** | Unsuccessful | The response makes an ineffective attempt. It contains very little elaboration, telegraphic or disconnected language, a severely limited vocabulary range, serious frequent errors, or minimal original language. |
| **0** | Invalid or nonresponsive | The response is blank, rejects the task, is not in English, is entirely copied from the prompt, is entirely unrelated, or consists of arbitrary keystrokes. |

# 5. Distinguishing Email Scores 4 and 3

This is the most important operational distinction.

## Score 4

A score-4 email is **generally successful**:

- the recipient can easily understand the reason for writing;
- the important requested points are adequately addressed;
- details are sufficient for the recipient to respond or act;
- organization and tone are appropriate;
- language errors are present only to a limited degree;
- the reader does not need to guess the intended meaning of important sentences.

## Score 3

A score-3 email is **partially successful**:

- the main purpose is generally understandable;
- one important point may be missing, vague, weakly developed, or difficult to interpret;
- social conventions may be inconsistent;
- language limitations may prevent parts of the message from being fully clear or effective;
- errors are noticeable enough to reduce task performance.

## Email 4-versus-3 Decision Test

Ask:

> Can the recipient understand the complete situation, the necessary details, and the requested action easily and respond appropriately?

- **Yes, with adequate detail and limited interference from errors:** likely 4.
- **Mostly, but an important part is incomplete, unclear, or weakened by language:** likely 3.

---

# 6. Email Evaluation Checklist

This checklist is an evaluator tool, not an official weighted formula.

## Communicative Purpose

- Is the reason for writing clear?
- Does the email perform the requested action?
- Are the major prompt requirements addressed?
- Is the requested response or next step clear?

## Elaboration

- Are the details specific enough?
- Does each detail support the purpose?
- Is anything important merely mentioned rather than explained?
- Could the recipient act based on the information provided?

## Coherence

- Is the information presented in a logical sequence?
- Are sentences connected?
- Are there distracting repetitions or unrelated details?
- Is the message complete?

## Social Conventions

- Is the tone suitable for the recipient?
- Are requests appropriately polite?
- Are complaints or criticism professionally expressed?
- Are greeting, closing, and organization appropriate?

## Language Facility

- Is there sufficient sentence variety?
- Is vocabulary appropriate for the situation?
- Are collocations natural?
- Do errors remain limited enough for the message to be easily understood?

# 7. Write for an Academic Discussion

## 7.1 Task Purpose

The test taker reads:

- a professor’s discussion question;
- short posts from other students.

The test taker then contributes a position to the discussion, supported by personal reasoning, experience, or knowledge.

## 7.2 Official Construct

The Academic Discussion task measures the ability to produce a multi-sentence response that:

1. clearly elaborates an argument for a position by responding to arguments and/or using information from the short posts;
2. is adequately supported, clear, and cohesive;
3. uses an appropriate range of grammar and vocabulary accurately;
4. follows English spelling, punctuation, and capitalization conventions.

## 7.3 Meaningful Contribution

The response should contribute something beyond simple repetition.

Acceptable methods include:

- agreeing with a student and adding a different reason;
- disagreeing and explaining the weakness of the argument;
- qualifying a student’s position;
- combining the two positions;
- introducing a new example, condition, consequence, or practical solution.

The writer is **not required** to:

- name another student;
- begin with “I agree with…”;
- choose one student’s position exactly;
- use a fixed template.

The requirement is to make a relevant contribution in the writer’s own words.

---

# 8. Academic Discussion: Official Scoring Dimensions

The Technical Manual’s task-specific feature table lists two principal construct areas.

## A. Content

Core question:

> Are the explanations, examples, and details relevant and sufficiently elaborated?

Disclosed feature examples include:

- number of sentences;
- discourse coherence;
- similarity or relevance to the prompt.

### Practical interpretation

Evaluate whether:

- the writer answers the professor’s actual question;
- the position is identifiable;
- the reasoning is explained;
- the example or detail supports the reason;
- the response advances the discussion;
- ideas follow a coherent progression.

A useful reasoning chain is:

\[
\text{Position}
\rightarrow
\text{Reason}
\rightarrow
\text{Example or evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{Explanation of relevance}
\]

A response may be relevant but still underdeveloped if it lists ideas without explaining them.

---

## B. Syntactic and Lexical Variety

Core question:

> Does the response use varied enough sentence structures and appropriate, idiomatic vocabulary?

Disclosed feature examples include:

- sentence variety;
- word frequency;
- correctness of collocations.

### Practical interpretation

Evaluate:

- control of sentence structures;
- use of logical relationships such as cause, contrast, concession, and result;
- appropriateness and precision of word choice;
- natural collocations;
- avoidance of repetitive or formulaic phrasing.

The goal is not maximum complexity. The goal is sufficient language range to express and connect the argument clearly.

---

## C. Accuracy Within the Holistic Rubric

The Academic Discussion feature table does not display a separate Accuracy/Errors row. However, grammatical and lexical accuracy clearly remains part of the official 0–5 rubric.

The score descriptors explicitly distinguish:

- almost no errors at 5;
- few errors at 4;
- noticeable errors at 3;
- accumulated errors at 2;
- serious frequent errors at 1.

Therefore, grammar and mechanics remain integral to the final holistic score even though ETS does not present them as a separately weighted Discussion dimension.

---

# 9. Academic Discussion: Score-Level Rubric

| Score | Official label | Technical interpretation |
|---:|---|---|
| **5** | Fully successful | The response is highly relevant and very clearly expressed. Explanations, examples, or details are well developed; sentence structures are varied and effective; vocabulary is precise and idiomatic; almost no meaningful lexical or grammatical errors occur beyond normal timed-writing slips. |
| **4** | Generally successful | The response is a relevant contribution whose ideas are easily understood. Explanations, examples, or details are adequately developed; syntax shows variety; word choice is appropriate; lexical and grammatical errors are few or limited enough not to reduce clarity materially. |
| **3** | Partially successful | The response is mostly relevant and mostly understandable. Part of an explanation, example, or detail may be missing, unclear, or irrelevant. The response shows some syntactic and vocabulary range, but noticeable lexical or grammatical errors reduce effectiveness. |
| **2** | Mostly unsuccessful | The response attempts to contribute, but ideas may be poorly developed, only partially relevant, or difficult to follow. Language range is limited, and accumulated errors may interfere with comprehension. |
| **1** | Unsuccessful | The response makes an ineffective attempt to contribute. It contains few coherent ideas, severely limited grammar and vocabulary, serious frequent errors, or minimal original language. |
| **0** | Invalid or nonresponsive | The response is blank, rejects the task, is not in English, is entirely copied from the prompt, is entirely unrelated, or consists of arbitrary keystrokes. |

---

# 10. Distinguishing Discussion Scores 4 and 3

## Score 4

A score-4 discussion response is **generally successful**:

- the position is relevant and understandable;
- the central reason is adequately explained;
- examples or details clearly support the argument;
- the response contributes meaningfully to the discussion;
- ideas are easy to understand on the first reading;
- language errors remain limited enough not to materially interfere.

## Score 3

A score-3 discussion response is **partially successful**:

- the position is mostly relevant and understandable;
- an explanation, example, or detail may be missing, unclear, weakly connected, or irrelevant;
- the contribution may repeat rather than develop;
- language errors are noticeable and reduce clarity or precision;
- the reader may need to reconstruct part of the reasoning.

## Discussion 4-versus-3 Decision Test

Ask:

> Is the position supported by a relevant, adequately explained reason or example, and can every important idea be understood easily?

- **Yes, with adequate development and limited interference from errors:** likely 4.
- **Mostly, but part of the support is missing, unclear, irrelevant, or linguistically difficult:** likely 3.

---

# 11. Academic Discussion Evaluation Checklist

This checklist is an evaluator tool, not an official weighted formula.

## Relevance and Position

- Does the response answer the professor’s exact question?
- Is the position clear?
- Does the response remain focused?

## Contribution

- Does the response add something beyond the existing posts?
- Does it respond to, qualify, extend, or challenge an argument?
- Is the contribution original rather than copied?

## Elaboration

- Is the main reason explained?
- Is there a relevant example, detail, consequence, or condition?
- Is the connection between the example and position clear?
- Is any important reasoning merely asserted?

## Coherence

- Do ideas progress logically?
- Are cause, contrast, concession, and result expressed clearly?
- Does the conclusion follow from the reasoning?
- Is any part contradictory or irrelevant?

## Language Facility

- Is there sufficient sentence variety?
- Is vocabulary appropriate and idiomatic?
- Are collocations natural?
- Are important sentences easy to understand?
- Do errors remain limited enough for the response to function successfully?

# 12. Practical Official-Style Scoring Procedure

Use this sequence when rating either extended response.

## Step 1: Check for Score 0 Conditions

Assign 0 only when the response is:

- blank;
- not in English;
- entirely copied from the prompt;
- entirely unrelated;
- a rejection of the topic;
- arbitrary keystrokes.

Otherwise continue.

## Step 2: Determine Whether the Task Is Functionally Accomplished

For Email:

- Is the communicative purpose achieved?
- Can the recipient understand what happened and what action is requested?

For Discussion:

- Is there a relevant position?
- Is there a meaningful contribution with support?

If the attempt is largely ineffective or difficult to interpret, consider 1–2.

## Step 3: Evaluate Elaboration

Determine whether the response is:

- effectively elaborated;
- adequately elaborated;
- partially elaborated;
- limited or irrelevant.

This is central to separating:

- 5 from 4;
- 4 from 3;
- 3 from 2.

## Step 4: Evaluate Language Facility

Consider:

- sentence variety;
- vocabulary range;
- idiomatic word choice;
- collocations;
- grammatical control;
- mechanical accuracy.

Do not count errors mechanically. Determine whether they are:

- almost absent;
- few or limited;
- noticeable;
- accumulated;
- serious and frequent.

## Step 5: Apply Task-Specific Criteria

For Email, additionally evaluate:

- politeness;
- register;
- organization;
- formulation of requests, criticism, apologies, or suggestions.

For Discussion, additionally evaluate:

- relevance to the online discussion;
- quality of the argument;
- relevance and adequacy of support.

## Step 6: Select the Best-Fitting Holistic Band

Use the overall pattern:

- **5:** fully successful;
- **4:** generally successful;
- **3:** partially successful;
- **2:** mostly unsuccessful;
- **1:** unsuccessful;
- **0:** invalid or nonresponsive.

Do not average unofficial dimension scores. Use the dimensions to support one holistic judgment.

# 13. What Does Not Automatically Determine the Score

The official rubrics do not separately award points for:

- using advanced vocabulary;
- writing multiple paragraphs;
- using a fixed template;
- beginning with “I think” or “From my perspective”;
- naming another student;
- explicitly agreeing with a student;
- including a conclusion phrase;
- writing a specific number of words in the Email;
- producing error-free English.

These choices matter only insofar as they improve or weaken:

- task achievement;
- elaboration;
- coherence;
- language facility;
- social appropriateness.

## Length

Longer is not automatically better. Extra length can help only when it adds relevant development.

A shorter, complete, coherent response may score higher than a longer response containing:

- repetition;
- unrelated ideas;
- unfinished sentences;
- unclear examples;
- accumulated errors.

## Templates

A template is acceptable when it supports natural communication. It becomes harmful when it:

- does not fit the situation;
- causes irrelevant repetition;
- produces an incorrect student reference;
- prevents a direct answer;
- uses language the writer cannot control.

# 14. Compact Scoring Reference

## Write an Email

| Dimension | Score-4 standard | Score-3 warning |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Communicative goal is adequately achieved | Goal is generally achieved but partly ineffective |
| Elaboration | Enough relevant detail to support the purpose | Important detail is missing, vague, or incomplete |
| Coherence | Easy to follow | Some part is unclear or weakly connected |
| Syntax/vocabulary | Sufficient variety and appropriate wording | Moderate range with noticeable limitations |
| Social conventions | Mostly appropriate tone, register, and formulation | Some inappropriate or ineffective conventions |
| Errors | Limited enough not to interfere materially | Noticeable enough to reduce clarity or effectiveness |

## Academic Discussion

| Dimension | Score-4 standard | Score-3 warning |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Direct, relevant contribution | Mostly relevant but partly unfocused |
| Position | Clear and easy to understand | Understandable but imprecise or delayed |
| Elaboration | Adequately explained reason/example/detail | Support is missing, unclear, weak, or irrelevant |
| Contribution | Adds meaningfully to the discussion | Repeats more than it develops |
| Syntax/vocabulary | Varied enough and appropriate | Some variety but noticeable limitations |
| Errors | Limited enough for easy understanding | Noticeable enough to weaken the reasoning |
