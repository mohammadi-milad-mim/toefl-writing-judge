## 1. Main Goal

Write a clear, relevant, and sufficiently developed contribution to the discussion.

A strong response should:

- answer the professor’s exact question;
    
- clearly present your own position;
    
- engage meaningfully with the discussion;
    
- contribute a new reason, distinction, example, or solution;
    
- explain how your reasoning supports your position;
    
- remain focused and logically organized;
    
- use clear and controlled English.
    

### Practical target

- **Time:** 10 minutes
    
- **Length:** approximately 120–150 words
    
- **Format:** usually one cohesive paragraph
    
- **Priority:** development and clarity, not complicated vocabulary
    

---

# 2. Do Not Force One Template

Different questions require different argumentative structures.

Use one of these five styles:

1. **Agree and Add**
    
2. **Build on One Idea**
    
3. **Balanced or Hybrid Solution**
    
4. **Qualified Disagreement**
    
5. **Independent Alternative**
    

Before writing, decide which style best matches your real position.

---

# 3. Quick Template Selection

|Situation|Best template|
|---|---|
|You agree with one student but have a different reason|Agree and Add|
|One student’s point is strong and can be extended|Build on One Idea|
|Both students identify valid concerns|Balanced or Hybrid Solution|
|You support one side but must answer a serious objection|Qualified Disagreement|
|Neither student addresses the most important issue|Independent Alternative|

---

# 4. Template Style 1: Agree and Add

## When to use it

Use this when:

- you agree with one student’s general position;
    
- you can add a different supporting reason;
    
- the other student raises a concern you can answer.
    

## Structure

> Position → agreement → new reason → explanation → example → opposing concern → response → conclusion

## Template

From my perspective, **[position]** would be the more effective approach. I agree with **[Student A]** that **[valid point]**, and I would add that **[your different reason]**. This matters because **[explanation]**. For example, **[specific situation and result]**. Although **[opposing concern]**, **[solution, limitation, or response]**. Therefore, **[balanced conclusion]**.

## Model response: Volunteer work

From my perspective, a reasonable volunteer-work requirement would benefit high school students. I agree with Maria that volunteering can make students more aware of other people’s needs, and I would add that it prepares them for future professional responsibilities. Volunteer activities require students to manage their time, communicate with unfamiliar people, and complete assigned tasks. For example, a student helping at a community event may need to coordinate with other volunteers and solve unexpected problems. Although students already have demanding schedules, schools could offer flexible opportunities during weekends or vacations. Therefore, a limited requirement would provide meaningful experience without placing an excessive burden on students.

# 5. Template Style 2: Build on One Idea

## When to use it

Use this when:

- one student presents a useful idea;
    
- you want to extend that idea rather than introduce a completely different argument;
    
- discussing the opposing point would add little value.
    

You do not always need to address both students equally.

## Structure

> Position → build on one student’s idea → explain the extension → example → broader implication

## Template

From my perspective, **[position]**. Building on **[Student A’s]** point about **[their idea]**, **[your extension]**. This is especially important because **[explanation]**. For example, **[specific situation]**. As a result, **[broader benefit or consequence]**. Therefore, **[final conclusion]**.

## Model response: Recorded lectures

From my perspective, professors should make most lectures available as recordings. Building on David’s point about helping absent students, recordings can also improve revision and understanding. Students often need to hear a complex explanation more than once before fully understanding it. For example, an engineering student preparing for an examination could replay the section explaining a difficult formula instead of relying only on incomplete notes. As a result, recordings would support not only students who miss class but also those who attend and need additional review. Therefore, recorded lectures should be treated as a learning resource rather than merely a replacement for attendance.

## Useful phrases

- Building on Sara’s point, …
    
- This argument can be extended further because …
    
- Another consequence of this idea is …
    
- This benefit is particularly important for …
    
- The same reasoning also applies to …
    

---

# 6. Template Style 3: Balanced or Hybrid Solution

## When to use it

Use this when:

- both students make valid points;
    
- the question presents two competing benefits;
    
- a combined policy would be more practical than choosing one extreme.
    

## Structure

> Recognize both sides → present hybrid position → explain how each part works → example → synthesis

## Template

From my perspective, both arguments identify important concerns, and a combined approach would be more effective than choosing either option completely. **[Part one of your solution]** would provide **[benefit A]**, while **[part two]** would preserve **[benefit B]**. For example, **[specific implementation]**. This arrangement would **[explain result]**. Therefore, **[balanced conclusion]**.

## Model response: Online and in-person classes

From my perspective, both arguments identify important needs, and a mixed system would be more effective than requiring every class to use the same format. Large lectures could remain online to provide the flexibility Omar describes, while smaller seminars could be held in person to support the relationships Lena emphasizes. For example, students could watch recorded lectures independently and attend one weekly discussion class for group work and direct contact with instructors. This arrangement would reduce unnecessary commuting without removing academic and social support. Therefore, a hybrid system would serve first-year students better than either a completely online or completely in-person program.

## Useful openings

- A balanced solution would be more effective than either extreme.
    
- Both positions raise valid concerns, and a combined policy could address them.
    
- The strongest approach would preserve the benefits of both options.
    
- Rather than choosing one format completely, …

# 7. Template Style 4: Qualified Disagreement

## When to use it

Use this when:

- you clearly support one side;
    
- the opposing concern is serious;
    
- you can acknowledge the concern without changing your final position.
    

The disagreement should focus on the point, not the student.

## Structure

> Position → main reason → explanation → example → acknowledge objection → explain why it does not overturn your position

## Template

From my perspective, **[position]** remains the stronger option because **[main reason]**. This is important because **[explanation]**. For example, **[specific situation and result]**. Although **[valid opposing concern]**, **[why it is limited, manageable, or outweighed]**. Therefore, **[restated qualified position]**.

## Model response: University internships

From my perspective, universities should require students to complete an internship because academic knowledge alone does not fully prepare them for employment. Internships allow students to apply theoretical concepts, communicate in professional environments, and identify skills they still need to develop. For example, a computer science student may understand programming but discover during an internship that teamwork and project planning are equally important. Although unpaid placements may be financially difficult for some students, universities could provide grants or accept paid part-time work as an alternative. Therefore, an internship requirement would remain valuable if universities implemented it flexibly and fairly.

## Ways to answer an objection

### Solve it

> Although the program may be expensive, universities could begin with a limited pilot.

### Limit it

> Although this may affect some students, it would not apply equally to everyone.

### Outweigh it

> Although the policy creates short-term inconvenience, its long-term benefits are more significant.

### Add a condition

> Although misuse is possible, clear rules could substantially reduce the risk.

### Offer flexibility

> Although one requirement may not suit everyone, students could choose from several alternatives.

# 8. Template Style 5: Independent Alternative

## When to use it

Use this when:

- you disagree with both students;
    
- both students overlook a more important issue;
    
- the question should be evaluated using a different criterion;
    
- you have a stronger alternative solution.
    

## Structure

> Identify missing issue → present alternative position → explain criterion → example → conclusion

## Template

From my perspective, neither position fully addresses the central issue, which is **[missing issue]**. The decision should depend primarily on **[your criterion or alternative]**. This matters because **[explanation]**. For example, **[specific situation]**. Focusing on **[your criterion]** would **[result]**. Therefore, **[independent conclusion]**.

## Model response: School technology spending

From my perspective, neither position fully addresses the central issue, which is how effectively the technology would be used. Buying more devices does not automatically improve education, while rejecting new technology may prevent students from developing useful digital skills. The decision should depend primarily on whether schools can provide suitable software and teacher training. For example, purchasing tablets would have little value if teachers only used them to display ordinary textbooks. In contrast, well-designed digital activities could provide immediate feedback and personalized practice. Therefore, schools should evaluate the quality of implementation rather than simply deciding whether technology is generally good or bad.

## Useful openings

- Neither argument fully addresses the central issue.
    
- The more important consideration is …
    
- The decision should depend primarily on …
    
- A different approach would be more effective.
    
- The discussion overlooks one important factor: …

# 9. Opening Styles

Avoid repeatedly beginning with:

- I think …
    
- I believe …
    

Use different natural openings.

## Personal-position openings

- From my perspective, …
    
- From my point of view, …
    
- As I see it, …
    

### Best default

> From my perspective, …

`As I see it` is natural but slightly more conversational. `From my perspective` is safer for general use.

## Direct argumentative openings

- A more effective approach would be to …
    
- The strongest option would be …
    
- A balanced policy would …
    
- The main advantage of this approach is …
    
- The central issue is not … but …
    
- Neither proposal fully addresses …
    

## Examples

> From my perspective, regulated AI use would be more effective than a complete ban.

> A balanced attendance policy would serve students better than a strict requirement.

> The central issue is not whether students use technology, but how they use it.

> Neither argument fully addresses the financial consequences of the policy.

---

# 10. Agreement Phrases

## Agree and add

- I agree with Sara that …, and I would add that …
    
- Daniel raises an important point about …, and another consideration is …
    
- Maya correctly emphasizes …, and this approach could also …
    
- Building on Alex’s argument, …
    
- The benefit Sara identifies is important, and a further advantage is …
    

## Do not merely repeat

### Student says:

> Internships provide practical experience.

### Weak

> I agree because internships help students gain experience.

### Better

> I agree that internships provide practical experience, and I would add that they help students determine whether a particular career suits them.

---

# 11. Development Formula

Develop one main reason using:

> **Reason → explanation → mechanism → example → result**

## Example

> Recorded lectures improve learning because students can review difficult explanations. This is especially useful in technical courses where a single missed detail can make later material difficult to understand. For example, a student could replay a demonstration before completing an assignment. As a result, the student would rely less on incomplete notes or secondhand explanations.

One developed reason is usually stronger than several shallow reasons.

---

# 12. Example Formula

A specific example should contain:

> **Person or group → situation → action → result**

## Weak

> For example, online classes are helpful.

## Strong

> For example, a student who works in the evening could watch a recorded lecture earlier in the day instead of missing the course entirely.

## Useful starters

- For example, a student who …
    
- Consider a situation in which …
    
- Suppose that …
    
- A university could, for instance, …
    
- This can be seen when …
    
- In my own experience, …
    

---

# 13. Counterargument Rule

When disagreeing, address the **point**, not the student.

## Less natural

> Although Andrew is right that students are busy, …

## Better

> Although students already have demanding schedules, …

## Less natural

> Emma is wrong because recordings do not reduce attendance.

## Better

> Although recordings may reduce attendance in some cases, professors can maintain participation through discussions and quizzes.

# 14. Five Complete Model Structures

## Style 1: Agree and Add

> From my perspective, **X** would be the better policy. I agree with **A** that **Y**, and I would add that **Z**. This matters because **explanation**. For example, **specific case**. Although **concern**, **response**. Therefore, **conclusion**.

## Style 2: Build on One Idea

> From my perspective, **X**. Building on **A’s** point about **Y**, **extension**. This is important because **explanation**. For example, **case**. As a result, **implication**. Therefore, **conclusion**.

## Style 3: Balanced Solution

> From my perspective, both arguments identify important needs, and a combined approach would be more effective. **Part A** would provide **benefit A**, while **Part B** would preserve **benefit B**. For example, **implementation**. Therefore, **balanced conclusion**.

## Style 4: Qualified Disagreement

> From my perspective, **X** remains the stronger option because **reason**. This matters because **explanation**. For example, **case**. Although **objection**, **response**. Therefore, **qualified conclusion**.

## Style 5: Independent Alternative

> From my perspective, neither position fully addresses **missing issue**. The decision should depend primarily on **new criterion**. This matters because **explanation**. For example, **case**. Therefore, **alternative conclusion**.

# 21. Safe Sentence Structures

## Addition

> Group projects develop teamwork, and they also prepare students for professional collaboration.

## Cause

> Recorded lectures are valuable because students can review difficult explanations.

## Result

> Students can review difficult material, so recordings may improve understanding.

## Contrast

> Online lectures provide flexibility, whereas in-person seminars support direct interaction.

## Concession

> Although online classes are convenient, first-year students may need regular face-to-face support.

## Condition

> If universities offer both formats, students can choose the option that best suits their needs.

## Relative clause

> Students who work part-time may benefit from flexible schedules.

# 22. Common Weak Patterns

## Weak: Forcing agreement

> I agree with Sara, although I actually support Robert’s position.

### Better

Choose a different template and state your actual position directly.

---

## Weak: Summarizing both students

> Sara says online learning is flexible. Robert says interaction is important.

### Better

> A hybrid system would preserve the flexibility of online lectures while maintaining direct interaction through weekly seminars.

---

## Weak: Repeating the same reason

> I agree that internships provide experience, and I would add that they give students experience.

### Better

> I agree that internships provide practical experience, and I would add that they help students evaluate possible career paths.

---

## Weak: Listing ideas

> It is cheaper, easier, faster, modern, and convenient.

### Better

> Its main advantage is accessibility. Students who live far from campus could attend classes without spending several hours commuting.

---

## Weak: Vague example

> For example, this helps many people.

### Better

> For example, a student with an evening job could watch a lecture earlier in the day instead of missing the course.

---

## Weak: Empty conclusion

> In conclusion, this is the best option.

### Better

> Therefore, a hybrid policy would maintain flexibility without sacrificing regular academic support.

# 23. Ten-Minute Test Routine

## 0:00–1:00 — Choose and plan

- Identify your position.
    
- Select one of the five templates.
    
- Write keywords for your reason, explanation, example, and conclusion.
    

## 1:00–8:30 — Write

Develop one clear argumentative direction.

Do not change templates halfway through unless necessary.

## 8:30–10:00 — Review

Check:

1. Is my position clear?
    
2. Did I choose a structure that fits my actual opinion?
    
3. Did I add something new to the discussion?
    
4. Did I explain my main reason?
    
5. Is my example specific?
    
6. Did I address the opposing point when useful?
    
7. Did I avoid unnecessary repetition?
    
8. Are my sentences complete and grammatically controlled?
    

---

# 24. Final Self-Check

## Task fulfillment

- Did I answer the professor’s exact question?
    
- Is my position unmistakable?
    
- Did I contribute a new idea rather than merely summarize?
    

## Template choice

- Does my chosen structure fit my real position?
    
- Did I avoid forcing agreement or disagreement?
    
- Is the response logically consistent from beginning to end?
    

## Development

- Did I explain how or why my reason matters?
    
- Did I provide a specific example?
    
- Does the example clearly support the argument?
    

## Engagement

- Did I engage with at least one idea from the discussion?
    
- Did I address the point rather than attack the person?
    
- Did I respond to the opposing concern when it was relevant?
    

## Language

- Does every sentence have a subject and verb?
    
- Are verb forms and plural nouns correct?
    
- Did I use natural transitions?
    
- Did I remove obvious spelling and punctuation errors?
    

---

# 25. Final Rule

Do not memorize one complete paragraph.

Memorize five argumentative routes:

1. **I agree and have another reason.**
    
2. **I can extend this useful idea.**
    
3. **Both views should be combined.**
    
4. **The objection is valid but manageable.**
    
5. **A more important issue has been overlooked.**
    

Choose the route first. Then write a focused response around one central idea.
